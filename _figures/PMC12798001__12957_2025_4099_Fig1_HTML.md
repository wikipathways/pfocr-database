caption: Key steps in histone Klac modification. A The Warburg effect has been observed
  across diverse biological systems, including both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms.
  This metabolic pathway is characterized by the cellular preference for converting
  glucose to pyruvate via glycolysis, which is subsequently reduced to lactate, even
  under oxygen-sufficient conditions, thereby facilitating rapid energy production
  and the accumulation of biosynthetic precursors. B The lactate generated during
  the Warburg effect serves as the fundamental substrate for Klac. It is first activated
  to form the high-energy donor lactyl-CoA, a reaction catalyzed by certain ATP-dependent
  enzymes such as ACSS2. This lactyl-CoA then acts as the direct co-substrate for
  lactate transferases, which catalyze the covalent modification of lysine residues
  on target proteins. C The Klac modification occurs when lactyl-CoA transfers its
  “lactyl-” group to specific lysine residues on histones (e.g., H3K18lac) and non-histone
  proteins. Catalyzed by lactyltransferases (“writers”) such as p300 and KAT2A, this
  process is metabolically dependent, with its levels directly influenced by fluctuating
  intracellular lactate concentrations. D The removal of Klac is catalyzed by “eraser”
  enzymes, including histone deacetylases (HDACs). A key mechanism involves HDAC1
  to HDAC3, which remove the lactyl group from lactylated histones (e.g., H3K18lac),
  reverting them to the unmodified state and thereby maintaining epigenetic plasticity.
  Klac lysine lactylation, ACSS2 Acetyl-CoA synthetase 2, H3K18lac Histone H3 lysine
  18 lactylation, p300 EP300 lysine acetyltransferase, KAT2A Lysine acetyltransferase
  2 A
