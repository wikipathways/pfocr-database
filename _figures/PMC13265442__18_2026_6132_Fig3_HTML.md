caption: Schematic diagram of protein ubiquitination. Ubiquitination is a key posttranslational
  modification that regulates protein stability, localization, and signaling by covalently
  attaching ubiquitin to target proteins through E1-activating enzymes, E2-conjugating
  enzymes, and E3 ligases. The number and linkages of ubiquitin molecules can be classified
  into several types. Monoubiquitination involves the addition of a single ubiquitin
  to one lysine site and regulates processes such as DNA repair, endocytosis, and
  trafficking. Multimonoubiquitination refers to the addition of single ubiquitins
  on multiple lysines of the same substrate, often linked to receptor internalization.
  Polyubiquitination generates ubiquitin chains with distinct linkages, which serve
  different cellular functions. K48-linked chains are the canonical signals for proteasomal
  degradation, whereas K63-linked chains mediate the DNA damage response, signal transduction,
  and autophagy. Other linkages, including K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, and linear M1,
  contribute to cell cycle regulation, immunity, and protein trafficking. This diversity
  of modifications constitutes the “ubiquitin code,” which ensures the precise control
  of protein fate and cellular pathways. Dysregulation of ubiquitination is closely
  associated with cancer, neurodegeneration, immune disorders, and viral infection
