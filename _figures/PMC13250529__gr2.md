caption: The biogenesis, release and uptake mechanisms of exosomes and their molecular
  constituents. Exosomes have a lipid bilayer membrane composed of surface proteins
  (tetraspanins, integrins, immunomodulatory proteins, etc.) and various lipid molecules
  (ceramides, sphingomyelin, PI, etc.). The luminal section of the exosome primarily
  comprises nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, miRNA, and lncRNA), proteins (heat shock proteins,
  cytoskeletal proteins, endosomal sorting complex required for transport components,
  and fusion proteins), as well as lipids, metabolites, and growth factors that are
  involved in exosome formation and function. Exosome biogenesis begins when the plasma
  membrane invaginates the luminal side of the cell to generate early endosomes. Extracellular
  constituents and cell surface receptors enter endosomes during the formation process.
  With the transfer of cytoplasmic substitutes from the nucleus, Golgi apparatus,
  endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria, a second luminal invagination occurs at
  late sorting endosomes, resulting in luminal vesicles containing these cytoplasmic
  cargos inside the MVBs. These MVBs can then combine with lysosomes to break down
  their contents and make them available for cellular recycling. However, in the exosome
  biogenesis pathway, these MVBs are transported to the plasma membrane, and exocytosis
  occurs, releasing exosomes from the cells into the extracellular matrix space. At
  the same time, exosomes are available for distant or nearby recipient cells to take
  up. Exosome uptake occurs in recipient cells through various mechanisms, including
  direct ligand-receptor interactions, caveolin‒clathrin-mediated endocytosis, plasma
  membrane fusion, and phagocytosis. Thus, their cargo is transferred to the nucleus
  when they enter exosomes, and Golgi apparatus transforms their functional characteristics.
  ER, endoplasmic reticulum; MHC, major histocompatibility complex; MVBs, multivesicular
  bodies; PM, plasma membrane
