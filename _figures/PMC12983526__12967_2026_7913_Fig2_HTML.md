caption: In the respiratory system, AHLs produced by Gram-negative bacteria activate
  TAS2Rs in airway epithelial cells, which are coupled with heterotrimeric G protein
  subunits. The gustducin G protein dissociates into its α, GANT3, and βγ subunits.
  The βγ subunits activate PLCβ2, leading to the release of Ca²⁺, which results in
  an increased Ca²⁺ concentration from inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3)-sensitive
  calcium stores. This activates the transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily
  M member 5 (TRPM5), altering the membrane potential (depolarization), and increases
  ciliary beat frequency (CBF) in the airway. Simultaneously, calcium signaling can
  further activate nitric oxide synthase (NOS), leading to the production of large
  amounts of NO. NO accelerates ciliary movement through the SGC and protein kinase
  G (PKG) pathways, enhancing the clearance of foreign particles and strengthening
  the physical barrier of the airways. TAS2R inhibit the production of cyclic adenosine
  monophosphate (cAMP) via Gα and phosphodiesterase (PDE). Notably, cAMP suppresses
  phagocytosis. Given that cAMP inhibits phagocytosis, when macrophages (MΦ) maintain
  a reduced cAMP level and are co-stimulated with agonists that elevate cAMP levels,
  the Gβγ subunit activates phospholipase C (PLC). This activation leads to the production
  of inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and calcium (Ca²⁺), which in turn triggers the generation
  of nitric oxide (NO). NO then activates SGC to produce cyclic guanosine monophosphate
  (cGMP), thereby directly enhancing phagocytosis. Moreover, NO signaling promotes
  the migration of MΦs toward airway epithelial cells, activating their phagocytic
  function to engulf pathogens. NO itself directly disrupts the cell membrane and
  organelles of invading pathogens, inhibiting their growth and reproduction
