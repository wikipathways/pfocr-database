caption: 'Methionine is obtained from various sources, such as diet and gut microbiota.
  Dietary methionine levels vary based on composition (e.g., higher in high-protein
  vs. vegan diets). An additional source of methionine is protein catabolism. Free
  methionine can be released either through non-specific protein degradation via autophagy
  or through the removal of the N-terminal methionine from newly synthesized proteins
  by aminopeptidase 2 (MetAP2). a) Methionine Cycle: Methionine is converted to S-adenosylmethionine
  (SAM) by methionine adenosyltransferase (MAT). SAM donates methyl groups to methyltransferases
  (MTs) and is converted to S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), which is processed into
  homocysteine by S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine hydrolase (SAHH). Homocysteine can be
  remethylated to methionine via methionine synthase (MS) or betaine-homocysteine
  methyltransferase (BHMT). b) Transsulfuration Pathway: Homocysteine is converted
  to cystathionine by cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) and further into cysteine by
  cystathionine γ-lyase (CTH). Cysteine is used for glutathione, taurine, and hydrogen
  sulfide (H2S) production. c) Salvage Cycle: Involves polyamine synthesis and methionine
  salvage through intermediates such as dcSAM, MTA, and MTOB.Methionine can be eliminated
  directly in food via oxidation. Synthetic biotics can be used to deliver enzymes
  that degrade methionine in the digestive system and prevent its entry into circulation.
  Bacterial enzymes such as methioninase and MetDC can degrade methionine and may
  be used either as recombinant proteins, genetically encoded tools, or delivered
  through microbiota.Instead of targeting methionine for degradation, inhibiting methionine
  release with MetAP2 inhibitors or blocking the methionine cycle with MAT2A inhibitors
  may offer alternative approaches.Figure created using BioRender'
