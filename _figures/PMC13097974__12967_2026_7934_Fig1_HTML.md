caption: 'The molecular structure and signaling pathway of HER3. the structure of
  HER3 comprises three parts: the extracellular domain (ECD) (composed of subdomains
  I-IV), the hydrophobic transmembrane region (TM), and the intracellular domain (ICD).
  The ICD includes the juxtamembrane domain, the tyrosine kinase domain (TKD), and
  the C-terminal tail. In the absence of ligands, the subdomains II and IV of HER3
  bind to each other to maintain the inactive state of HER3. When ligands such as
  NRGs bind to HER3‘s I and III subdomains, the connection between the II and IV subdomains
  is disrupted, which leads to the exposure of the dimerization arm of the II subdomain,
  causing the molecular conformation shifting to an activated state. The exposed dimerization
  arm can interact with other receptor tyrosine kinases to form a heterodimer, where
  HER2 comes as the most common heterodimerization partner for HER3, given that HER2
  has no known ligand and remains a conformation capable of dimerization. Upon forming
  a heterodimer with HER3, HER2 (or other dimerization partners) phosphorylates tyrosine
  residues in the C-terminal tail of HER3, as well as undergoes autophosphorylation
  in the kinase domain. The receptor dimer then recruits cytoplasmic regulatory proteins
  and signaling molecules, thereby activating downstream signaling cascades that are
  crucial for cancer cell survival and proliferation, including the PI3K-AKT, RAS-RAF-MEK-MAPK,
  and JAK-STAT pathways. Notably, the C-terminal signaling tail of HER3 contains six
  tyrosine phosphorylation sites, which allows the direct binding to the p85 regulatory
  subunit of PI3K. This makes HER3 the only member of the HER family which is capable
  of directly activating the PI3K-AKT pathway'
