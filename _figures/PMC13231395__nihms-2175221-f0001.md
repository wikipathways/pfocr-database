caption: Pharmacotherapies for inflammation-associated pathways reported to be dysregulated
  in suicidal behaviour. Arachidonic acid is converted into prostaglandins and other
  mediators capable of causing inflammation, primarily by COX-2. Therefore, drugs
  that inhibit this conversion are one of the most potent choices of treatment. Glucocorticoid
  receptor (GR) resistance hastens the negative feedback of cortisol, thereby leading
  to its overstimulation. In response to stress, activated microglia release proinflammatory
  cytokines (PIC), which, in conjunction with cortisol, further stimulate the kynurenine
  pathway, favouring the conversion of tryptophan (TRYP) to quinolinic acid (QUIN)
  via kynurenine. Consequently, antidepressants such as tricyclics, lithium, or ketamine
  either prevent microglial activation or the release of PIC by them. QUIN elicits
  inflammatory signals and acts as an NMDA receptor agonist, leading to oxidative
  stress and excitotoxicity. Ketamine acts as an NMDA antagonist and prevents QUIN-mediated
  over-stimulation of its receptors. Autonomic inflammation reflex stressors lead
  to the release of catecholamines and acetylcholine via the stimulation of the sympathetic
  and parasympathetic nervous systems. The released neurotransmitters are capable
  of binding to cholinergic and adrenergic receptors on immune and endothelial cells.
  Adrenergic activation of these cells causes the release of cytokines, which in turn
  leads to inflammation. Lithium and cytokine antagonists are potential treatment
  arms wherein the former can shunt the release of GSK-3, and the latter can oppose
  or neutralise inflammatory cytokines
