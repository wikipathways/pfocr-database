caption: 'High‐dose radiotherapy and immunotherapy induce immune activation and the
  abscopal effect. Below the figure the following: High‐dose radiotherapy (RT), particularly
  when delivered in hypofractionated regimens or as stereotactic body radiotherapy
  (SBRT), can act as an immunological adjuvant by inducing immunogenic cell death
  and promoting systemic antitumor immune responses. Upon exposure to high‐dose RT
  (> 8 Gy), tumor cells undergo apoptosis, releasing danger‐associated molecular patterns
  (DAMPs), including HMGB1 and nucleic acids. These DAMPs activate the cytosolic DNA
  sensor STING (stimulator of interferon genes) pathway in antigen‐presenting cells,
  resulting in the production of type I interferons (IFN‐I), IL‐6, and TNF, and triggering
  a DNA‐driven innate immune response. Mature dendritic cells (DCs) uptake tumor‐associated
  neoantigens and migrate to lymph nodes, where they prime naïve T cells. Cross‐presentation
  of neoantigens via MHC‐I leads to the activation of antitumor CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes
  (CTLs), while MHC‐II presentation stimulates CD4+ helper T cells, further amplifying
  the immune response through the secretion of IL‐2, IFN‐γ, and other cytokines. Activated
  CD8+ T cells traffic to both irradiated and non‐irradiated tumor sites, mediating
  the abscopal effect—tumor regression outside of the radiation field. However, tumor
  immune evasion mechanisms such as PD‐1/PD‐L1 and CTLA‐4 immune checkpoints can inhibit
  T‐cell activation. Notably, RT upregulates PD‐L1 expression on tumor cells, which
  paradoxically increases tumor susceptibility to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB).
  Combining RT with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti‐PD‐1 or anti‐CTLA‐4 antibodies)
  can restore T‐cell function and synergistically enhance antitumor immunity, leading
  to improved systemic responses'
