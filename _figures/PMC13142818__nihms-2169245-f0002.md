caption: a, In mouse syngeneic models of melanoma, sympathetic nerves release catecholamines
  that stimulate β1-adrenergic receptors (β1-ARs) on CD8+ T cells, leading to increased
  T cell exhaustion and accelerated tumour growth. Blocking this pathway with β-blockers
  such as atenolol reduces T cell exhaustion and enhances responses to immune checkpoint
  inhibitors (ICIs)100. b, Tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) and myeloid-derived
  suppressor cells (MDSCs) express α2-adrenergic receptors (α2-ARs). When an agonist
  activates these receptors, adenylate cyclase (AC) is inhibited, thereby blocking
  the immunosuppressive cAMP–cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) signalling
  pathway. Consequently, TAMs become more effective at stimulating antitumour CD4+
  and CD8+ T cells, whereas MDSCs undergo apoptosis, collectively enhancing antitumour
  immunity94. c, Hypoxia in the tumour microenvironment (TME) induces the release
  of signalling molecules such as adenosine, prostaglandins (prostaglandin E2 (PGE2))
  and catecholamines. These molecules bind to Gαs-coupled G-protein-coupled receptors
  (GPCRs) (for example, adenosine A2a receptor (ADORA2A), prostaglandin E receptor
  (EP) and β-AR), activating cAMP–protein kinase A (PKA) pathways. This activation
  promotes T cell exhaustion, characterized by impaired cytokine production (interleukin-2
  (IL-2), interferon-γ (IFNγ), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)) and reduced cytotoxic
  activity. Gαs-coupled GPCR signalling diminishes T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and
  enables tumour cells to evade immune responses74
