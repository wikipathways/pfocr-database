caption: 'The roles of microbial amino acid metabolites and receptors in IBD. Tryptophan
  is metabolized into kynurenic acid, indole and its derivatives by specific gut microbes,
  which influence IBD through multiple mechanisms: 1) acting on pregnane X receptor
  (PXR), aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2), or G protein-coupled
  receptor 35 (GPR35) in intestinal epithelial cells; (2) inhibiting the mTOR signaling
  pathway in T helper cell 17 (Th17) or activating AhR in T cells. Glutamate is catalyzed
  by several microbiota into gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which alleviates intestinal
  barrier dysfunctions by activating GABA type A receptors (GABAARs) or inhibiting
  the AMPK pathway. Parabacteroides merdae and Clostridium sporogenes can metabolize
  branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) to produce branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs),
  which enhances Tregs activity or promoting sustained mTOR signaling in B cells,
  leading to protection from colitis. Arginine is metabolized by some bacteria to
  produce polyamines (e.g., putrescine, spermine, and spermidine), however, these
  polyamines have different effects on IBD. Histidine is catalyzed into histamine
  and imidazole propionate (ImP) by specific microbiota. The distinct roles of microbiota-derived
  histamine have been identified in mice with colitis. Several strains from the Lactobacillus,
  Enterococcus, and Providencia genera catalyze the conversion of Tyrosine into tyramine
  by tyrosine decarboxylase. Enterococcus-derived tyramine hijacks α2A-adrenergic
  receptor (ADRA2A) in intestinal stem cells to exacerbate colitis. This Figure was
  created by MedPeer (medpeer.cn)'
