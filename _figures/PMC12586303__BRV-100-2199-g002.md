caption: Evidence for epigenetic regulation of sex changes. (A) In some cases, epigenetic
  changes have been found to be associated with epigenetic changes. This is for example
  the case for monoecious cucumbers (Cucumis sativus), in which high temperature and
  long‐day treatments induce gain in DNA methylation at flowering and phytohormone‐pathway‐related
  genes, resulting in lower female/male flower ratios (Lai et al., 2018). We speculate
  that environmental changes can induce epigenetic changes and changes in the activity
  of phytohormone‐related genes, eventually leading to sex changes. It is however
  not clear whether such effect is direct, and/or whether there is a cause–consequence
  or an interactive effect between epigenetic changes and changes in phytohormone‐pathway‐related
  genes. (B) Sex changes sometimes occur in hotspots of labile branches, such as in
  female individuals of Populus trichocarpa, which under natural conditions can display
  hotspots of inflorescences with both male and hermaphrodite flowers (Stettler, 1971).
  This evidence suggests that the altered sexual phenotype is mitotically inherited
  from a progenitor cell displaying this phenotype, which easily can be explained
  by the mitotic stability of epigenetic modifications. (C) Removal of DNA methylation
  can induce changes from males to individuals with intermediate sex expression in
  Silene latifolia and Diospyros lotus (Janoušek et al., 1996; Akagi et al., 2016).
  In S. latifolia, removal of DNA methylation likely repressed the expression of the
  female‐suppressing gene in males (not shown in the figure). For D. lotus, we suggest
  that the removal of DNA methylation induces the activation of a feminizing flowering
  gene and/or phytohormone‐related genes
