caption: 'Mechanobiological regulation of CAT. (a) The tumor microenvironment disrupts
  all components of Virchow’s triad: endothelial injury, altered flow, and hypercoagulability.
  Cancer cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) compress vessels, and elevated
  interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) further contributes to abnormal perfusion, endothelial
  damage and barrier dysfunction [21, 22]. Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TGF-β)
  activate endothelium and promote leukocyte adhesion [23]. EVs from cancer cells
  further amplify endothelial inflammation and procoagulant signaling. Tumor-induced
  vascular remodeling disrupts blood flow and shear stress, fostering thrombosis [24,
  25]. (b) Tumor expansion induces compressive stress, elevated IFP, and ECM stiffening,
  which serve as key mechanical stimuli [21, 22]. These forces activate mechanosensitive
  pathways (e.g., PIEZO1-mediated Ca²⁺ influx), triggering downstream signaling (e.g.,
  YAP/TAZ) and upregulating procoagulant factors like TF [26, 27]. Both cancer and
  stromal cells respond to these cues, promoting thrombogenic conditions. (c) Cancer
  cells, platelets, CAFs, and RBCs cooperate in thrombus development. Cancer cells
  express procoagulant TF and release EVs that amplify thrombogenicity [28]. CAFs
  secrete TGF-β and EVs, modulating endothelial and platelet activation. Platelets
  adhere to damaged endothelium via P-selectin and vWF, forming thrombotic foci [29,
  30]. Under low shear stress, RBCs aggregate, contributing to stasis. Endothelial
  cells express adhesion molecules (e.g., E-selectin, ICAM-1), facilitating leukocyte
  recruitment [31]. (d) TF, vWF, and tumor-derived EVs initiate coagulation cascades
  and promote endothelial inflammation [25]. Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α)
  and chemokines (e.g., PF4, CXCL8) enhance endothelial activation and leukocyte recruitment.
  Platelets release PF4 and other mediators, priming neutrophils for NETosis [32,
  33]. The resulting DNA-histone webs entrap circulating platelets and RBCs, stabilizing
  fibrin-rich thrombi [16]. These interactions form a positive feedback loop that
  sustains cancer-associated thrombosis. Created with BioRender.com'
